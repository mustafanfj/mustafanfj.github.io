Research

One question, asked in three places where a wrong answer costs something. Select a project to see how it works.

  1. A frozen small VLM answers each question through four evidence sources. Reliability weights fit on a small development split decide which source to trust, and the model abstains when its calibrated confidence is low. Up to +7.0 points with no fine-tuning.

    Let the model say “I’m not sure”

    75.1% correct, answering every question.

    From the paper's held-out EVAL split; the threshold is chosen on DEV.

    Code and paper notes

  2. A drone sends the smallest message that still lets the ground station act in time. A 1 kB semantic alert keeps 73% of the best image's balanced recall with 105× fewer bytes, and meets its deadline on links down to 0.1 Mb/s.

    Code

  3. Seven VLMs diagnose 1,219 crop-leaf images independently. The strongest from each family then debate by name and may change a verdict only by citing new visual evidence. Debate leaves the panel's vote unchanged but lifts its weakest member by 12.1 points.

    Paper Code

  4. TwinView, a dual-view handheld imaging system that returns a diagnosis, its severity, the visual evidence and a recommended action in seconds. Validated on farms in Al Ain. I founded it and lead the research.

  5. An agent plans a short, question-specific sequence of tools for each reef question, and an independent verifier checks every answer against its evidence. +3.7 points over GPT-5 zero-shot on a 100-image CoralVQA development set; frozen, it answered 98.3% of 3,000 questions across 500 images.

    Code

  6. Domain-specific features and a neural network regressor score underwater image quality without a reference image: the first filter in CoralSense AI, which pairs it with CoralAgent-TRACE and is supported by a UAEU–Environment Agency Abu Dhabi SDG research grant.

  7. A stacking ensemble over multi-domain handcrafted features for assessing coral health from underwater images.

Why this work

I study Computer & AI Engineering at UAE University, graduating in May 2027, and I am looking for a master's place in computer vision.

Most of my work asks one question: can we trust what a vision-language model says it sees? I try to answer it with mechanisms rather than prompts: models that must cite their evidence, that debate one another by name, that are weighted by how reliable they proved on held-out data, and that are allowed to abstain.

I test these ideas where mistakes cost something: over flooded streets, where every byte on a weak drone link counts; on crop leaves, with a diagnosis a farmer in Al Ain can act on; and on coral reefs, where bleaching has to be judged from murky underwater images.

In 2026 that became seven papers, four as first author, with released code for each main system. This summer I joined the eBRAIN Lab at NYU Abu Dhabi to study why compressed perception models break down in fog and rain.
